The same total order, executed either all at once or split into equal slices over time with partial book replenishment between slices.

06Impact vs. Timing Risk Trade-off

Splitting an order into more, smaller slices reduces the instantaneous price impact of each slice — but it also stretches out the execution horizon, during which the price can move for reasons unrelated to your own trading. That exposure is timing (market) risk.

07Formula Reference
Average Execution Price
AEP = Σ(price_i · qty_i) / Σ(qty_i)
Volume-weighted average of the prices at which the order actually filled, walking the book level by level.
Slippage
Slippage = (AEP − best quote before the order) × side
The extra cost incurred purely from walking past the best price into deeper, worse-priced levels.
Arrival-Price Shortfall (Implementation Shortfall)
Shortfall = (AEP − arrival mid-price) × side
The total cost versus the price that prevailed at the moment the decision to trade was made — includes both the cost of crossing the spread and the cost of walking the book.
Book-Referenced VWAP
VWAP_book = Σ(price_i · size_i) / Σ(size_i), over all displayed bid + ask levels
A liquidity-weighted "center of gravity" of the pre-trade book — distinct from the mid-price when the book is imbalanced between bids and asks.
Price Impact
Impact = (new mid-price after the sweep − arrival mid-price), in ticks and bps
How far the quoted market moved simply because your order consumed displayed liquidity — distinct from slippage, which measures your own execution cost rather than the market's new quote.
Participation Rate
Participation = Order Size / ADV
How large the order is relative to a typical day's trading volume — a common proxy for how much attention an order should draw and how much it should be worked over time.
Square-Root Impact Model
Impact (bps) = α · (Q / ADV)^β
A widely used educational approximation (β ≈ 0.5 is the classical "square-root law"). α and β are configurable here purely for exploration — real trading desks calibrate these from historical execution data.
08Market Impact vs. Market Risk
Why Splitting an Order Trades One Risk for Another

Market impact is the cost your own order creates by consuming displayed liquidity — the more size you demand right now, the further you walk into the book, and the worse your average fill price gets. A single large block order concentrates all of this cost into one instant.

Splitting that same order into smaller slices, executed over time, reduces each individual slice's impact — smaller orders don't need to walk as far into the book, and if the book has any resilience (liquidity replenishing between trades), later slices may trade against a partially refreshed book.

But spreading execution out over time introduces timing risk (sometimes called market risk in this context): the price can move for reasons that have nothing to do with your own trading — news, other participants, broad market moves — while your order is still being worked. The longer the execution horizon, the more that uncertainty accumulates, roughly growing with the square root of elapsed time for a random-walk-like price process.

This is the central trade-off in optimal execution: trade fast and pay more in impact but face less timing risk, or trade slow and reduce impact but accept more exposure to unpredictable price moves in the meantime. Neither extreme is universally "better" — the right balance depends on urgency, risk tolerance, and how confident you are that immediate execution cost outweighs the risk of the market moving against you while you wait.

Limitations of This Simulator

This is a simplified, illustrative model. Real order books move continuously as other participants add, cancel, and trade — this simulator uses a static snapshot that only changes because of your own order (with a simple, deterministic replenishment assumption between slices). It ignores adverse selection, information leakage, other traders reacting to your activity, and the fact that real liquidity is often not fully visible in the displayed book. Treat every number here as a teaching aid, not a forecast of real trading costs.
