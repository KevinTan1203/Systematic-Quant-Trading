07Tick Size & Market Quality
What tick size is

The tick size is the minimum price increment on which quotes and trades may be expressed. It defines the discrete grid of legal prices. A $0.01 tick on a $50 stock is 2 bps per tick; the same tick on a $5 stock is 20 bps — so economic tick size depends on price level as well as the absolute increment.

Small-tick vs large-tick regimes

Large ticks force a wider minimum spread (at least one tick). That can increase displayed depth at each level — because improving the price costs a full tick — and may simplify queue priority. But it can also keep the spread artificially wide relative to volatility, raising costs for liquidity takers.

Small ticks allow finer quote competition and potentially tighter spreads. They can also fragment depth across more price levels, intensify cancel/replace activity, and make time priority at a given price less “valuable” because stepping ahead by one tick is cheap. Neither regime is universally optimal; effects depend on liquidity, volatility and trader mix.

Queue priority & quote competition

With price-time priority, the first order at the best price is first in line. When the tick is large, joining the queue at the touch is often the main way to compete. When the tick is small, liquidity providers may improve by one tick instead of waiting in a long queue — tightening the spread but thinning size at each level.

Sub-penny quoting & nuance

In some venues and contexts, sub-penny pricing exists for certain order types or dark venues even when the lit tick is one cent. That can create complex interactions between displayed and non-displayed liquidity. This simulator uses a single discrete tick grid for clarity and does not model sub-penny exceptions — a deliberate simplification.

Empirical market-quality studies often find mixed results when regulators change tick size: spreads, depth, and trading costs need not all improve together. Treat the relationships you observe here as mechanistic illustrations inside a stylised model, not as policy conclusions.
