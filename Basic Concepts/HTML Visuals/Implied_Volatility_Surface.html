13Understanding the IV Surface
Volatility Smile, Skew & Term Structure
Volatility Smile

Implied volatility often rises for both out-of-the-money puts and calls relative to at-the-money options — a "smile" — because tail outcomes (large moves in either direction) are priced with extra volatility relative to what a single lognormal model would imply.

Volatility Skew

When downside (put) strikes carry persistently higher IV than equivalent upside (call) strikes, the smile becomes asymmetric — a "skew." This is frequently linked to demand for downside protection and to the empirical tendency of equity-like assets to fall faster than they rise, though the model here simply imposes a linear+quadratic shape, not a derived equilibrium.

Term Structure

IV can differ substantially by expiry: near-term options may reflect a specific upcoming event, while longer-dated options average over more uncertainty. An upward-sloping term structure means longer maturities carry higher ATM IV; downward-sloping (inverted) term structure is often associated with near-term stress.

The Surface Itself
IV = f(K, T)
A single constant volatility cannot simultaneously price every strike and maturity consistently with observed option prices — the market instead implies a different volatility for each (K,T) pair, which together form the surface.
How Traders Read the Surface

High IV — options may be more expensive in premium terms, but high IV does not by itself mean options are "overpriced"; it may simply reflect genuinely elevated expected variability.

Low IV — options may be cheaper in premium terms, but low IV does not by itself mean options are "underpriced."

Steep put skew — often read as elevated demand for downside protection, or asymmetric pricing of crash risk.

High short-dated IV — often associated with a known near-term event or elevated near-term uncertainty.

Flat surface — suggests more uniform volatility expectations across strikes and maturities.

Implied volatility is market-implied pricing, back-solved from option premiums — it is not a direct forecast of what realized volatility will turn out to be.
