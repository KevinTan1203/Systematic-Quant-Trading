07Formulas
Log Spread & Z-Score
S_t = ln(A_t) − β_t · ln(B_t)
Z_t = (S_t − μ̂_t) / σ̂_t
μ̂, σ̂ estimated over a rolling window. β_t is either fixed, true, or rolling OLS of ln A on ln B.
Half-Life (OU approximation)
ΔS_t = a + b S_{t−1} + ε_t  ⇒  κ̂ ≈ −ln(1+b)  ⇒  HL ≈ ln(2)/κ̂
Estimated on the full-sample spread path (educational). Requires −1 < b < 0 for mean reversion.
Trading Rule
Z > +Z_entry → short spread (short A, long β·B)
Z < −Z_entry → long spread (long A, short β·B)
Exit when |Z| < Z_exit
P&L (unit notional on A)
Long-spread daily P&L ≈ r_A − β · r_B − costs on position changes
Costs = (txn + slippage) bps applied to each leg when the position changes.
08Concepts
Correlation vs cointegration

Correlation measures co-movement of returns over a window — it can be high even when the price spread wanders without bound. Cointegration is a stronger notion: a linear combination of levels (here log prices) is stationary, so the spread mean-reverts. Pairs trading needs mean reversion of the spread, not merely correlated returns.

Stationarity, mean reversion & half-life

A stationary series fluctuates around a constant mean with finite variance. The half-life estimates how many days the spread takes to close half of a shock under an OU model. Very long half-lives mean capital is tied up; very short ones may be noise dominated after costs.

Hedge ratio & z-score

The hedge ratio β scales the short leg so that the residual is closer to stationary. Using rolling OLS adapts to changing relationships but introduces estimation lag and noise. The z-score standardises the spread so entry/exit rules are comparable across regimes of volatility.

Why historical relationships break

Regime shifts, corporate actions, changing business mix, index reconstitution, or a structural break in the cointegrating vector can all destroy a previously reliable spread. Backtests over a single calm regime overstate edge. Always ask what economic force anchors the spread — and what would invalidate it.
