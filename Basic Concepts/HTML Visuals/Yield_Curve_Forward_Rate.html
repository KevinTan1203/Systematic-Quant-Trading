07Formula Reference
Discount Factor (Annual Compounding)
DF(T) = 1 / (1 + y(T))^T
y(T) is the annually compounded zero rate for maturity T (in years). This is the educational convention used by default.
Discount Factor (Continuous Compounding)
DF(T) = e^(−y(T) · T)
Available via the compounding selector. Continuous rates are common in theoretical work and some derivatives pricing.
Simply-Compounded Forward Rate
(1 + y₂)^T₂ = (1 + y₁)^T₁ · (1 + f)^(T₂ − T₁)
⇒ f = [ (1 + y₂)^T₂ / (1 + y₁)^T₁ ]^(1/(T₂−T₁)) − 1
f is the forward rate from T₁ to T₂ implied by today’s zero curve (annual compounding).
Approximate Instantaneous Forward
f_inst(T) ≈ − d(ln DF) / dT (finite-difference approximation)
Computed between adjacent maturities as a practical discrete approximation to the instantaneous forward rate.
Par Yield (Annual Coupon)
c = (1 − DF(T)) / Σ DF(tᵢ)
Coupon rate that makes a bond with annual coupons and maturity T price at par given the discount curve.
08Understanding the Term Structure
Normal, Flat & Inverted Curves

Normal (upward-sloping) curves are the historical average shape: longer-dated rates sit above shorter-dated rates, reflecting term premium and expectations of growth/inflation.

Flat curves appear when the market expects little change in the policy rate path or when a transition between regimes is underway.

Inverted curves (short rates above long rates) have often preceded recessions. They typically price expected future rate cuts. Inversion does not guarantee a recession, but it is one of the more reliable leading indicators in the historical record.

Humped shapes can arise from a combination of near-term rate expectations and a longer-term term premium.

Spot vs Par vs Forward Rates

Spot / zero rates are the yields on zero-coupon instruments. They are the fundamental building blocks of the discount curve.

Par yields are the coupon rates that make a fixed-rate bond price exactly at par. They are what you typically observe in the coupon bond market and are often quoted as “the yield curve” in practice. Par yields lie close to but are not identical to zero rates.

Forward rates are the future rates implied by today’s curve that make investors indifferent between locking in a long-term zero and rolling shorter zeros. They are break-even rates, not forecasts that the market “expects” in a statistical sense, and they are certainly not guarantees of future realised rates.

Why Forward Rates Are Not Guarantees

A 1-year forward rate starting in 5 years is simply the rate that equates the return of a 6-year zero with the return of a 5-year zero rolled into a 1-year investment. It embeds expectations, risk premia, and liquidity preferences. Realised future rates can (and usually do) differ substantially from today’s implied forwards. Treating the forward curve as a pure forecast is a common source of confusion.

Discount Factors & Pricing

Once you have a complete set of discount factors, any fixed cash-flow instrument can be valued as the sum of those cash flows multiplied by the corresponding DFs. This is why the zero curve (or its equivalent discount curve) is the central object in fixed-income pricing. Changing a single zero rate changes the entire set of implied forwards and the value of every instrument that depends on that part of the curve.
