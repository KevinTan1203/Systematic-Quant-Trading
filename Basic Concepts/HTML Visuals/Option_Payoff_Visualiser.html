07Formula Reference
Per-Leg Intrinsic Value
Call: max(S − K, 0) Put: max(K − S, 0)
What the option is worth if exercised immediately at expiry — zero if out-of-the-money.
Combined Payoff
Payoff(S) = Σ legs [ direction × quantity × multiplier × intrinsic(leg, S) ]
direction = +1 for long, −1 for short. Stock legs contribute direction × quantity × S instead of an intrinsic-value term.
Net Premium
Net Premium = Σ legs [ −direction × quantity × multiplier × premium ]
Negative = net debit (you paid cash up front); positive = net credit (you received cash up front).
Profit & Loss
P&L(S) = Payoff(S) + Net Premium
This is the number that actually reflects profitability — the payoff chart alone systematically overstates your result by ignoring what you paid (or understates it, if you collected a credit).
Breakeven(s)
Solve P&L(S) = 0
Since P&L is piecewise-linear in S with kinks only at option strikes, breakevens are found exactly by checking for sign changes between consecutive strikes (plus the S→∞ asymptote), not by approximation.
08Payoff vs. P&L — Why Premium Matters
Payoff Is Not Profit

The payoff of an option position at expiry is simply what it's worth if you exercised or closed it right then — for a call, max(S−K,0). It says nothing about whether the trade made money, because it ignores what you paid (or received) to enter the position in the first place.

Profit & Loss is payoff net of that initial cash flow. A long call with a payoff of $3 at expiry is not a $3 profit if you paid $5 for it — it's a $2 loss. Conversely, a short put that expires worthless has zero payoff, but if you collected $4 in premium when you sold it, your P&L is a $4 gain.

This is why every serious risk or profitability discussion about an option strategy has to start from P&L, not payoff — and why this tool deliberately shows both charts side by side rather than collapsing them into one.

Reading Breakevens, Max Profit, and Max Loss

Breakeven is the underlying price at which P&L is exactly zero — below it (or above, depending on direction) the strategy loses money; on the other side, it makes money.

Max profit and max loss describe the best and worst outcomes across the entire range of possible underlying prices at expiry. Strategies built purely from long options (calls, puts) or vertical spreads typically have a clearly bounded max loss (limited to premium paid or spread width). Strategies involving a naked short option — sold without an offsetting long option further out — can carry theoretically unlimited loss, which is exactly what the "illustrative capital requirement" section above is trying to make visible.

Terminal Payoff vs. Theoretical (Pre-Expiry) Value

The payoff and P&L charts describe the position at expiry — the only moment an option's value is purely intrinsic. Before expiry, an option also carries time value, which depends on volatility, time remaining, and interest rates. Toggling "Theoretical Value Mode" replaces each still-alive leg's terminal intrinsic value with its full Black-Scholes price at the chosen valuation date, so you can see how the position's current mark-to-market value differs from its eventual expiry payoff — the gap between the two curves is the position's remaining time value.
