09Understanding Leverage & Liquidation
Leverage, Initial Margin, and Maintenance Margin

Leverage lets you control a position larger than your own capital by borrowing the rest. 10× leverage on $1,000 of equity can control a $10,000 position.

Initial margin is the capital you must post to open that leveraged position — typically notional ÷ leverage. Maintenance margin is a lower threshold: once your equity (initial capital plus or minus unrealized P&L) falls to this level, the exchange or broker will start to liquidate the position to protect itself from your losses exceeding your posted capital.

Liquidation

Liquidation is the forced closing of a leveraged position once equity drops to the maintenance margin level. At that point you typically lose all or nearly all of the margin backing that position — leverage doesn't just amplify gains, it amplifies losses in exactly the same proportion, and can wipe out your posted capital far faster than an unleveraged position ever could.

Funding

Many leveraged/perpetual products periodically exchange a funding payment between longs and shorts to keep the contract's price anchored near the underlying spot price. When the funding rate is positive, longs typically pay shorts (and vice versa when negative). Funding is a slow, ongoing drag or boost on equity — usually much smaller per period than price moves, but it compounds over time the longer a position is held.

Why High Leverage Makes Small Moves Matter So Much

At 2× leverage, the price needs to move roughly 50% against you before your maintenance margin is breached. At 100× leverage, the Leverage vs. Liquidation Distance chart above shows that threshold shrinks to well under 1%. The underlying asset doesn't need to do anything dramatic — ordinary daily volatility can be enough to trigger liquidation at high leverage, which is precisely why leverage is often described as a tool that turns manageable price noise into an existential risk for the position.

Important Assumptions & Disclaimers

This simulator uses one illustrative, simplified convention for liquidation and margin ratio calculations. Real exchanges and brokers differ significantly in practice: some use isolated vs. cross margin, tiered maintenance margin rates that change with position size, insurance funds, partial liquidation, liquidation fees/penalties added on top of maintenance margin, and margin ratio conventions that are the inverse of the one used here. None of the figures in this tool should be treated as matching any specific exchange's actual liquidation engine — always consult your broker or exchange's official documentation for the rules that actually apply to your account.

Funding is modeled as a simple constant daily rate for illustration; real funding rates typically float and are paid at fixed intervals (e.g., every 8 hours) rather than continuously.
