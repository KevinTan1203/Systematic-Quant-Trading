07Formula Reference
Value at Risk
VaRα(R) = −q1−α(R) = −inf{x : P(R ≤ x) ≥ 1−α}
A positive VaR is reported as a loss magnitude. At 99% confidence, only 1% of outcomes are worse than −VaR on the return scale.
Expected Shortfall (CVaR / AVaR)
ESα(R) = −E[R | R ≤ q1−α(R)]
Average loss given that you are already in the VaR tail. Sensitive to how bad the bad cases are — not only the threshold.
Horizon scaling (simple)
μh = μ · (h/252)   ·   σh = σ · √(h/252)
Standard square-root-of-time scaling used here for illustration. Real P&L may need full revaluation and serial dependence.
Student-t standardization
R = μh + σh · √((ν−2)/ν) · Tν
Scale factor matches variance to σh² for ν > 2. Lower ν fatten tails while keeping the same variance.
Currency loss
Loss_$ = V₀ · VaRα   (and likewise for ES)
Linear translation from return quantile to portfolio-value terms (no convexity / gamma effects in this lab).
08Educational Explanation
What VaR does — and does not — tell you

VaR answers: “How bad can it get with probability α?” It is a quantile of the P&L/return distribution. It does not say how severe losses are beyond that threshold. Two distributions can share the same VaR and have very different crash severity.

  • Not subadditive in general → can discourage diversification in pathological cases.
  • Silent on tail shape beyond the quantile.
  • Easy to game if the distribution is manipulated just beyond the cutoff.
Why Expected Shortfall captures tail severity

ES averages all outcomes in the worst (1−α) mass. If crashes get worse, ES rises even when the VaR threshold is unchanged. Under standard conditions ES is coherent (including subadditive). Regulators (e.g. FRTB) moved from VaR toward ES partly for this reason.

Normal vs Student-t: fat tails

Matching mean and variance is not enough for risk. A Student-t with small ν puts more probability on extreme moves. At 99% and beyond, t-based VaR/ES can exceed Normal figures by a wide margin — visible in the comparison table and confidence sweep.

Model inventory in this lab
  • Normal — Gaussian with μh, σh.
  • Student-t — variance-matched heavy tails with degrees of freedom ν.
  • Historical-style — synthetic sample from a Gaussian scale-mixture (fat tails + mild skew control via kurtosis boost).
  • Monte Carlo — large Normal sample (sampling noise only); converges toward analytical Normal quantiles as N grows.
